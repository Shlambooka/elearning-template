Feedback loops in everyday life

Module 1 of 4 25%

You've seen the theory and the classic examples. Now let's spot feedback loops in things you already do. Click each example to reveal what's going on.

You drink coffee → caffeine blocks sleepiness → you feel alert → you drink less coffee later in the day. The system pushes back against "too much caffeine" by making you *want* less. That's negative feedback.

But the morning habit itself can spiral: the more tired you are → the more coffee you need → the worse your sleep → the more tired you are. That's positive feedback on the *habit*.

Real systems often contain both, layered.

More 5-star reviews → the app appears higher in search → more people download it → more reviews. That's positive feedback: the output (reviews) feeds the input (visibility), which feeds the output.

If the app suddenly gets buggy, the reverse can also snowball: bad reviews → lower ranking → fewer users → the app gets *less* attention and may decline faster. Still positive feedback — just pushing in the other direction.

You set 100 km/h. The car goes up a hill → speed drops → the engine accelerates → speed returns to 100 km/h. Downhill → speed rises → the engine eases off → speed returns to 100 km/h.

The system constantly pushes *against* any change from the target. That's textbook negative feedback — the same mechanism as a thermostat, just with wheels.

The pattern to remember

When you spot a loop, ask one question: does the response push against the change, or push along with it? Push back → negative. Push along → positive. The label is about direction, not whether the outcome is good.
